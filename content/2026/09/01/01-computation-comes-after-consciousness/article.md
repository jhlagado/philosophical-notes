# Computation Comes After Consciousness: Gödel, Lucas and Penrose on the Limits of Artificial Mind

By John Hardy

The computer on my desk can be described twice without contradiction. In one description it is silicon, metal, electrical current and heat. In the other it is bits, instructions, calculations and answers. The physical account explains the event; to understand why the result was this particular answer, I inspect the program. The answer belongs to a formal scheme.

Engineers establish the relation between the descriptions. They design components whose physical states can be treated reliably as symbols, arrange transitions that implement formal operations, and set standards under which a result is correct or mistaken. The machine realises the scheme in matter. Its computation depends on that scheme.

The usual computational account of mind starts with computation in nature. Matter processes information, biological complexity eventually produces brains, and consciousness appears when computation reaches sufficient depth. Human-built computers continue a process already present in the universe.

I think the explanatory order runs the other way. Nature supplies causal structure and regular change. Conscious reason identifies states, assigns them formal roles and judges their transformation under rules. Ink is physically necessary for a sentence on paper, but chemistry does not make the marks into words.

The priority concerns explanation and norms; chronological order is irrelevant to it. A computer can keep running in an empty room, and a spacecraft can execute instructions long after contact with its designers has been lost. Neither needs an observer attending to every transition. The relevant dependence lies in the history and standards that make those transitions instances of a particular operation, with a right result and possible errors.

Every known case of explicit computation belongs to human practice. The principle applies more widely: any rational subject capable of establishing symbols, rules and standards could create a computational practice.

I am arguing about explanatory adequacy, not for a second substance. Consciousness may depend entirely on physical processes. Yet if computation already relies on the capacities to recognise symbols, follow rules, grasp meaning and distinguish a result from a mistake, it cannot explain those capacities merely by presupposing them.

## Physical change and computation

The word *computation* carries several meanings. A computer scientist can study an abstract formal relation. An engineer can describe a mechanism that realises it. A biologist can model a cell as processing information, while a physicist may describe the evolution of a system as a computation. These uses need not stand or fall together.

Formal relations may be discovered; my argument is neutral on whether they are also made. It asks which formal structure a particular physical sequence instantiates, and what makes its states symbols with correct and incorrect transformations.

[Under permissive implementation criteria](https://consc.net/papers/rock.html), an ordinary open physical system can be described as realising any finite-state automaton over a suitable finite interval. The ease with which such mappings can be found exposes the problem. John Searle draws the larger conclusion: syntax is assigned by an observer, so the physical facts alone do not determine which computation a system implements.

A stone changes continuously in temperature, pressure and microscopic structure. An interpreter could select a sequence of those changes and map it onto the states of a simple program. Only that act of interpretation distinguishes the mapping from alternatives based on other physical features.

A digital computer seems different because it was built to support a particular family of mappings. Its gates respond systematically to alternative inputs, and its state transitions preserve the counterfactual structure of the formal operations. If I enter different numbers into a calculator, it produces correspondingly different outputs. The temperature history of a stone lacks that engineered organisation.

A [mechanistic account](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199658855.001.0001) grounds computation in function and counterfactual organisation. On this view, a physical system computes when it is organised to manipulate distinguishable vehicles according to a rule, and when its responses to alternative inputs preserve the structure of that rule. These constraints distinguish a calculator from a mapping imposed on a stone.

The appeal to function relocates the philosophical problem. An artefact has a computational function because people designed and use it for one. Its electronic states count as vehicles under standards established through human practice. Biological mechanisms invite another account: evolution and the organism's continued organisation may explain their functions. That possibility gives life an important role, but it does not yet turn a biochemical response into a formal symbol or an experienced meaning.

At this point the computational explanation divides. Thin computation means causally organised state transition, identified without semantics. It may be an objective property of mechanisms, but by itself it contains no account of meaning, correctness or experience. Thick computation includes symbols, rules and standards of success. It resembles thought because it has acquired those features from rational practice or from a prior account of normativity.

A world without conscious interpreters could contain the relevant physical regularities, mechanisms and living organisms. Calling all of that computation in the thin sense changes no physical fact. My stronger claim concerns computation as a formal, norm-governed activity. Without subjects capable of treating something as a sign, a rule or a result, nature contains causal order but no computation of this kind.

## Turing's exact boundary

Alan Turing gave effective procedure a mathematical form. A Turing machine reads and writes symbols according to a finite table of instructions. A universal machine can simulate any machine whose procedure can be specified in that form. The particular hardware may vary; the formal operations remain the same.

Turing's analysis also established a limit. No general effective procedure can decide in every case whether an arbitrary program running on an arbitrary input will eventually halt. Faster processors and larger memories make many computations practical that were once too expensive. They do not supply a decision procedure for the undecidable case.

The halting problem proves nothing directly about consciousness. It does not show that human beings can decide every question a computer cannot. Here it imposes discipline on claims about scale: formal procedure has limits in principle, and hardware cannot remove a boundary set by the logical character of the procedure.

Gödel's incompleteness theorems bring us closer to the distinction between formal procedure and understanding. They concern proof inside a formal system and truth considered from a standpoint outside it.

## Gödel, proof and truth

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, strengthened by Rosser, shows that any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal system strong enough to express elementary arithmetic is incomplete: it contains an arithmetical sentence that it can neither prove nor refute. The sentence is constructed in relation to that particular system. There is no single proposition that defeats every possible formal system.

The familiar Gödel sentence roughly asserts its own unprovability within the system. Consistency prevents the system from proving it, while the original construction requires a stronger condition to prevent the system from proving its negation. Rosser's construction obtains both results from consistency alone. Philosophical extensions become fragile when they treat the mathematician's recognition as though consistency alone guaranteed it.

The move from unprovability to mathematical truth requires another condition. If we accept the system as sound for the intended interpretation of arithmetic, we can reason at the meta-level and recognise why the relevant sentence is true even though the system cannot derive it. Gödel's second theorem rules out an easy escape: a consistent system of the required strength cannot prove its own consistency using only its own resources.

Proof is a sequence of expressions licensed by formal rules. Mathematical truth concerns what those expressions mean under an interpretation. The rules determine whether a proposed proof belongs to the system; they do not establish their own soundness or select the interpretation that makes their expressions about natural numbers. Several philosophies of mathematics can accommodate the difference between derivation within a system and reasoning about that derivation.

Formal proof gains its value from the fact that a valid derivation can be checked without relying on the private insight of the person who wrote it. The mathematician who chooses axioms, interprets the symbols and assesses whether the system preserves truth is already working at a level the derivation presupposes.

## Lucas and the machine on trial

In his 1961 essay *Minds, Machines and Gödel*, Lucas asks the mechanist to specify a machine that completely captures a mathematician's reasoning. Once the machine has been specified as a consistent formal system, its Gödel sentence can be constructed. The system cannot prove that sentence. A mathematician who understands the system and is entitled to accept its consistency can recognise the reason the sentence is true.

The mechanist can add the Gödel sentence as a new axiom. The enlarged system can prove the earlier Gödel sentence and has a new one of its own. Lucas compares a mind with each particular machine proposed as its complete model. Whenever the mechanist fixes the model, the mathematician considers that model from outside and constructs the corresponding challenge.

The claim that a human mathematician can “see” the Gödel sentence is true carries the argument's greatest liability. It requires confidence in the consistency or soundness of the formal system. People make mathematical mistakes, accept false premises and fail to detect contradictions. We have no general entitlement to declare ourselves consistent.

The mechanist has other replies. Perhaps the formal system implemented by a human mind is [too complex for the person to identify](https://doi.org/10.5840/monist196751112). A person could then instantiate a formal procedure without being able to discover which procedure it is, leaving the relevant Gödel sentence unavailable. Actual mathematical output is finite and fallible, so a formal system might reproduce everything a person ever proves without capturing an ideal of mathematical truth. A learning architecture also need not resemble a fixed list of axioms: its procedures and stored results can change with further input.

These objections block the claim that Gödel proved mechanism false. A harder problem remains. Mathematical practice includes examining axioms, correcting errors and adopting stronger systems. People can formalise those revisions in a larger system, which then becomes a new object of assessment. This open-ended movement does not prove that mathematical thought is non-algorithmic. It does prevent us from identifying the derivations of a fixed system with the practice that assesses them unless a further argument closes the gap.

Lucas's argument therefore works best as a challenge to a proposed identity. If a mechanist claims that mathematical understanding is exactly this algorithm, the algorithm can be examined for limits that understanding may recognise. The mechanist must then explain the movement between formal standpoints without assuming the prior understanding that makes revision rational.

## Penrose and mathematical understanding

Roger Penrose pushes this argument beyond a machine that mechanically enumerates proofs. In *The Emperor's New Mind* and *Shadows of the Mind*, he argues that no sound algorithm can account for mathematical understanding. His target includes learning procedures whose internal organisation may be too complicated for anyone to express as a short list of axioms.

For Penrose, understanding involves grasping what a formal system is about. A mathematician does more than produce a string that passes a proof checker. The mathematician follows the intended interpretation, assesses whether the axioms are acceptable and recognises whether the rules preserve truth. Once an algorithmic procedure has been made explicit and its soundness accepted, that act of understanding can reach beyond the procedure to the corresponding Gödelian statement.

Mathematical practice complicates Penrose's inference. Working mathematicians do not usually search mechanically through every permitted string until a proof appears. They rely on conjecture, analogy, diagrams, failed attempts and insight. The finished proof may be mechanically checkable even when no useful mechanical description of its discovery is available. [The difference between discovery and checking](https://math.stanford.edu/~feferman/papers/penrose.pdf) does not, by itself, establish that mathematical thought could never be represented computationally.

Soundness remains the hinge. Penrose considers an ideal of mathematical understanding whose errors are correctable against an impersonal standard. Individual mathematicians often fall short, but the standard lets them distinguish a mistake from a warranted result. His Gödelian conclusion becomes conditional: if a mathematician can recognise the soundness of a proposed algorithmic account of that mathematician's reasoning, the algorithm cannot exhaust the understanding used in that recognition.

The conditional claim falls short of proving that all minds exceed all machines, but it locates the burden precisely. A formal system has rules for producing conclusions. Understanding includes the capacity to ask whether those rules preserve truth about the subject matter. Turning that capacity into another formal rule shifts the question to the soundness of the enlarged system. An algorithm whose identity or soundness could never be known escapes the Gödelian challenge by placing the claimed identity beyond the mathematician's assessment.

Penrose goes further. He argues that a future physics must include non-computable processes and that such processes participate in consciousness. This conjecture is separate from the Gödelian case. Gödel does not establish the existence of such physics, and escaping algorithmic description does not turn an event into an act of understanding. Randomness supplies no meaning merely by departing from determinism.

Penrose leaves us needing an account of the physical basis of understanding if understanding really does outrun algorithmic procedure. Sentience raises another problem, one that non-computability alone cannot answer.

## Sentience as a different question

Intelligence, understanding and sentience are often treated as positions on a single scale, although each names a different question. Intelligence can be assessed through capacities such as solving a problem or adapting to new evidence. Understanding concerns a subject's grasp of reasons and meanings. Sentience concerns the presence of experience, the fact that there is something it is like to be that subject.

David Chalmers sharpens the problem by distinguishing functional explanation from conscious experience. A complete physical account may explain discrimination, report, attention, neural activity and behaviour. It still has to explain why any of this has a felt character: why there is something it is like for the subject. Chalmers calls this the hard problem of consciousness. Computation is well suited to describing structure and function, which is precisely why success at those tasks does not settle the further question.

The same problem applies to an algorithmic account of sentience. A formal specification can describe state transitions, outputs and dispositions. To infer subjectivity, we need a bridge principle showing why that organisation entails a point of view. Functionalism supplies one possible bridge by identifying mental states with their causal roles. The identification is a philosophical premise; computation does not prove it.

Non-computability offers no shortcut. A physical event outside the class of Turing-computable processes need not feel like anything. Random events are not tiny experiences. Penrose's argument about understanding, even if accepted, leaves open why understanding is conscious and why any physical process has a first-person aspect.

Our knowledge of other people also lacks deductive proof. We recognise them as fellow subjects through shared bodies, expression and participation in a common form of life. This recognition is a rational, fallible judgment.

Artificial systems present different evidence. A language model can produce fluent descriptions of pain, grief or selfhood because its training captures statistical structure in human language. The output is evidence of linguistic competence, but the output alone does not establish whether any experience accompanies its production. Behaviour gives us reasons for attribution; it never turns subjectivity into a directly observable property.

## Life and original normativity

Life appears to challenge the priority I have claimed. Organisms existed before formal mathematics, and a bacterium responds to its environment without waiting for a human interpretation. If those responses already constitute computation, then computation precedes conscious reason.

A cell continuously produces and maintains the components that constitute it as a bounded unity. Maturana and Varela called this organisation *autopoiesis*. Metabolism, repair and regulation contribute to the continuation of the organism. Other changes damage its organisation or bring it to an end.

No appeal to consciousness is needed to state this difference. A bacterium need not experience a desire to survive. Its chemistry nevertheless contains a real asymmetry between the processes under which the living organisation continues and those under which it disintegrates. The norms are primitive and biological: viable or non-viable, repaired or destroyed.

This may be where physical differences first acquire significance within a system's own organisation. A change in the environment counts differently according to its effect on the organism's continuation. Signals occur within a history of metabolism, exposure and repair. The life of the organism supplies the conditions under which those differences have consequences for it; a computational description captures relations among them.

Autopoiesis does not solve consciousness. Many self-maintaining processes may have no experience, and a theory must still explain how biological normativity becomes a point of view. Biological significance does not yet amount to a formal rule or a symbol recognised as such. The order is therefore different from the computational story: living organisation supplies primitive stakes, conscious life supplies a point of view, and rational practice makes symbols and rules explicit. Calling the first stage computation gives a computational description to an organisation whose norms arose from life; it does not show that computation produced the later stages.

Nothing here privileges carbon. An artificial subject may be possible, but its possibility would depend on more than the size of a program or the fluency of an interface. We would need an account of how the system constitutes and maintains itself, how events bear on its own continued existence, and how that organisation gives rise to a point of view. Calling it a computer would leave those questions unanswered.

## The explanatory debt

Computational models reproduce capacities associated with human intelligence. They classify images, generate language, discover useful patterns and solve difficult problems. Their success tells us how much of intelligent behaviour can be captured through formal modelling, and therefore something real about intelligence, language and representation. It also increases the temptation to treat successful modelling as an ontology of mind.

A computational theory of consciousness still owes three explanations. It must say which of the many formal patterns mapped onto a physical process gives that process its computational identity. It must account for the meaning of its states and the standards under which a transition is correct. It must explain why the resulting organisation is accompanied by experience.

Scale can extend behavioural range and support more complicated internal organisation. It cannot pay these conceptual debts on its own. Uninterpreted state changes do not select their own symbols. A fixed formal system does not establish its own soundness. A successful performance does not contain a proof that the performance is lived from within.

The computational account faces the dilemma encountered at the beginning. Thin computation describes causal organisation but does not contain meaning or experience. Thick computation includes symbols, rules and correctness, but those notions depend on norms grounded in living organisation and made explicit through rational practice. Increasing scale may enlarge a system's abilities; it does not decide which horn explains consciousness.

Computation describes structures through which thought becomes explicit and reproducible. A machine can continue the formal operations after its designers have left the room. A bare physical description does not say why those operations count as this rule, this meaning and this answer. Before there can be a program in that full sense, something must already count as a sign, a rule and a reason for a subject.
